### Introduction   

This week focuses on the early stage of the project: discovery and ideation. The goal is to explore possible directions for our web application prototype and begin understanding both the client’s needs and the target user group. Rather than committing to a single idea immediately, this stage encourages broad thinking and experimentation.
A key part of this stage is identifying who the users are and what they truly need. The client brief emphasizes not only building a functional platform but also creating an engaging experience that encourages users to return. Therefore, understanding user motivations, behaviors, and pain points becomes essential in shaping meaningful design directions.
By connecting user needs with the client’s goals, I can evaluate which ideas are more likely to create both value and engagement within a specific community. Idea generation also involves early evaluation of feasibility and implementation constraints.

---
### Initial Ideas  

During the ideation process, I explored several potential community concepts:  

1. A study-focused community for university students   
2. Anonymous emotional support community  
3. Creative accountability community   
4. A travel planning and experience-sharing hub  

At this stage, I aimed to generate multiple ideas rather than refining one. This helped avoid attachment to a single direction and allowed comparison between different possibilities.
Generating diverse ideas also helps reveal different design opportunities and challenges. Each concept has different trade-offs between user value, engagement, and complexity.

---
### Idea Evaluation  

Each idea targets a different user group with distinct needs, motivations, and challenges.  

---
Study community (University students) 

This idea targets university students who need structured support for studying, such as sharing notes, managing deadlines, and staying motivated. Their key pain points include procrastination and lack of accountability.
While this aligns with user needs, many existing platforms already address similar problems, making long-term differentiation challenging.   

---
Anonymous emotional support community (Young adults / stressed users) 

This idea focuses on young adults who may experience stress, anxiety, or emotional pressure and prefer a safe, anonymous space to express themselves. 
Their needs include emotional validation, privacy, and a sense of belonging. Designing for anonymity could increase participation, but also requires careful moderation and trust-building.  

---
Creative accountability community (Students / young creatives) 

This idea targets students or young creatives who want to build consistent habits such as drawing, writing, or designing. Their main challenge is maintaining motivation over time.
A community system with check-ins and peer encouragement could strongly support engagement and retention, aligning well with the brief.

---
Travel community (Young travelers / planners)  

This idea focuses on young travelers who are planning trips and looking for shared experiences, tips, and recommendations. Their needs include reliable information, inspiration, and personalized advice.  
While this idea provides clear value, many existing platforms already dominate this space. Therefore, creating a unique and tailored experience would be essential to stand out.   

---

Through this evaluation, I realized that a strong concept must balance three key factors: a clearly defined user group, a meaningful user need, and the ability to create an engaging and unique experience that supports long-term retention.  

---
### Next Steps  

Moving forward, I plan to refine these ideas through discussion. When selecting a final concept, feasibility, user needs, and interest will be key factors.
I will also explore user research methods, such as analysing existing platforms and user behaviour, to ensure the design is based on real insights rather than assumptions.

---
### Reflection   

This week highlighted the importance of not rushing into a single idea. By exploring multiple directions, I now have a clearer understanding of what makes a strong concept.
A successful design must not only meet functional requirements but also create a compelling experience that encourages users to return.
Overall, this stage helps guide more informed design decisions in later phases.
